What actually
converts
on a landing page.

Fewer surprises and clearer structure consistently beat clever design on the page that actually has to convert a visitor.

By Santiago Payá de la Cruz• June 30, 2026• 7 min read

A landing page has one job, unlike a homepage that has to serve several audiences and purposes at once. That single-purpose focus is exactly what most landing pages fail to actually commit to, adding navigation, secondary offers and design flourishes that dilute the one action the page exists to drive. Most of the underperformance traced back to a landing page has less to do with the copy and more to do with this basic lack of focus.

One offer, one action, no navigation

The single highest-leverage structural decision is removing the main site navigation from the page entirely. Every link that isn't the conversion action is an exit path, and a visitor who arrived specifically to consider one offer doesn't need a way to browse the rest of the site from that page. This one change alone often produces a measurable conversion lift on its own, independent of any other optimisation.

The headline has to answer one question immediately

What is this, and is it relevant to me, needs to be answerable within the first few seconds without scrolling. A clever headline that requires reading the whole page to understand costs conversions before a visitor ever reaches the parts of the page actually making the case. Clarity beats cleverness here more decisively than almost anywhere else in marketing copy.

Social proof placed where doubt actually happens, not just at the top

A testimonial or logo strip at the very top of the page, before any doubt has had a chance to form, does less work than the same proof placed right after the specific claim or price point most likely to trigger hesitation. Mapping where a visitor's doubt actually peaks, usually right before or after pricing information, and placing proof there specifically outperforms scattering it evenly across the page. Session recordings, where available, are a practical way to see exactly where visitors hesitate or scroll back up to re-check something, and they often reveal friction points that no amount of guessing from a design meeting would have surfaced.

One call to action, repeated, not several competing ones

A page offering "Book a call", "Download the guide" and "Sign up for the newsletter" as equally weighted options usually converts worse on all three than a page with one clear action repeated at natural points down the page. Choice, even a small amount of it, measurably reduces conversion when the goal is one specific action, a well-documented pattern in conversion research.

Why repetition of the same action outperforms variety

Repeating the same call to action after every major section, rather than introducing it once and hoping a visitor scrolls back up, meets people at whatever point they've actually made up their mind, which varies from person to person. This costs nothing in complexity and consistently outperforms a single, well-placed button that assumes everyone decides at the same point in the page.

What decorative design elements actually cost

Auto-playing video backgrounds, complex animations, and stock photography chosen for aesthetic fit rather than relevance all add load time and visual noise without adding to the actual case being made. None of these are inherently bad, but each one needs to earn its place by supporting the conversion action, not just making the page look more polished. This structural thinking is exactly what goes into every landing page we build, before a single visual decision gets made. Getting the structure right first, then designing around it, produces a page that converts even before it looks particularly polished.
