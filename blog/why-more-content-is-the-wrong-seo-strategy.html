"Publish more"
isn't a strategy.
It's an activity.

Content volume gets treated as a proxy for SEO progress. Here's why that substitution quietly backfires for most businesses.

By Santiago Payá de la Cruz• June 9, 2026• 7 min read

A content calendar with two posts a week feels like progress, and it's an easy thing to point to in a status update. Whether it's actually the right use of a limited budget is a separate question, and for a lot of businesses, particularly smaller ones without a large team or existing authority, the honest answer is no. The uncomfortable part is that this often only becomes clear after months of publishing with little to show for it, by which point the sunk cost of the existing calendar makes the shift harder to justify internally, even when the evidence is clear.

Volume without strategy dilutes rather than compounds

Content that doesn't map to a genuine gap in what a site already covers, and doesn't interlink meaningfully with related pages, adds pages without adding topical depth. Search engines and AI models both reward coherent coverage of a subject over a large but scattered archive, which means a high volume of loosely related content can genuinely work against the topical authority a business is presumably trying to build.

Thin content actively works against a site, not just neutrally

A large share of pages that add little unique value doesn't just fail to help, it can drag down how a search engine or a model assesses the overall quality of a site, since the average quality across all indexed pages is part of what gets evaluated. Publishing a weak page isn't a neutral action with no cost, it's a decision with a real, if often invisible, downside.

What actually moves the needle instead

Fewer pieces, each genuinely thorough, targeting a specific, realistic keyword with real search intent behind it, interlinked properly with the rest of the site's coverage on that topic, consistently outperform a larger volume of shallower content. This isn't a call to publish less overall, it's a case for treating each piece of content as a deliberate investment rather than a box to check on a calendar.

When volume genuinely does help

Large e-commerce catalogues, marketplaces, and sites where genuine long-tail search demand exists across thousands of specific product or use-case variations are the exception, since the underlying demand for that volume of distinct pages is real. The distinction is whether each new page serves a genuine, distinct search need, not whether the total count looks impressive on a content report.

The internal pressure that pushes toward volume anyway

A content calendar with a fixed weekly quota is easy to report on and easy to hold a team accountable to, which is exactly why it persists even when it's quietly working against the actual goal. Replacing a volume target with a quality bar, is this genuinely the best answer to a real question, is harder to measure in a status update but far more likely to produce results worth reporting on in the first place, which tends to matter more to leadership than the calendar looking full, once the actual traffic and lead numbers get compared side by side.

A more useful question than "how much should we publish"

What specific gap does this piece fill that isn't already covered, and what would make it the best available answer to the question it's targeting, rather than simply another entry in a growing archive. Answering that honestly for every piece before it gets written filters out most of the content that would have diluted rather than built topical authority in the first place. It's a harder filter to apply than simply hitting a publishing target, which is exactly why so many content calendars default to volume instead.
