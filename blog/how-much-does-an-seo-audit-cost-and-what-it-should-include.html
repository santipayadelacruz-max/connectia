A useful audit is not a PDF spat out by a tool. It is a person looking at your site and telling you what to fix first and why.

By Santiago Payá de la Cruz•October 26, 2026•8 min read

What an SEO audit is

It is an analysis of your site from a search engine's point of view: technical health, content, links, authority and competition. The goal is to identify what holds back your rankings and in what order to fix it.

An audit without an action plan is just a list of problems.

What drives the price

Site size, technical complexity, number of markets and languages, depth of competitor analysis and whether implementation help is included. A ten-page site does not need what a shop with thousands of products does.

That is why you should compare scope, not only the figure.

What it should include

A professional audit covers:

  • Crawling and indexing.
  • Speed and mobile experience.
  • Structure, titles and headings.
  • Content and cannibalisation.
  • Internal and external links.
  • Structured data.
  • Competitor analysis.
  • Local SEO if relevant.
  • A prioritised action plan.

Automated versus manual

Automated tools detect technical errors, but they do not understand your business or prioritise. A professional interprets data, discards false alarms and proposes an order of work.

Be wary of reports listing hundreds of warnings without saying which ones matter.

How it should be delivered

A clear document with findings, evidence and recommendations ordered by impact and effort, plus a call to explain it. Ideally with a roadmap for the first weeks.

Ask whether implementation help is included or only the diagnosis.

When an audit makes sense

Before a redesign, after a traffic drop, when starting with a new provider or when you do not know where to begin. Also as a first step before a retainer.

It is a good way to see a consultant's method before committing.

Where it usually goes wrong

The most frequent errors:

  • Treating an automated report as an audit.
  • Not asking for an action plan.
  • Not prioritising.
  • Not measuring the effect of changes.
  • Auditing once and never again.

How to tell a good one from a bad one

A good audit has priorities: fix this first, this next, this can wait. A bad one is a list of 200 'errors' where 90 per cent do not matter.

And a good audit ends with a conversation, not just an email. I want you to understand what I hand you.
