"Impressive"
isn't the goal.
Converting is.

A website built to impress and a website built to convert are sometimes the same thing, and often quietly aren't.

By Santiago Payá de la Cruz• December 1, 2026• 7 min read

"We want a website that really impresses people" is a common brief, and it's aiming at the wrong target more often than it sounds, since impressiveness and effectiveness aren't automatically the same thing even though they get treated that way. Visual impressiveness and conversion effectiveness overlap sometimes, but they're genuinely different goals with different success criteria, and optimising hard for one can actively work against the other in ways that aren't obvious until the traffic and conversion data eventually comes in.

What impressive design often actually costs

Elaborate animations, heavy custom illustrations, and visually dense layouts that photograph well for a design portfolio often add real load time, complicate navigation, and bury the specific information a visitor actually came looking for underneath layers of unnecessary visual flourish. A visitor doesn't leave a site because it wasn't impressive enough, they leave because they couldn't quickly find what they needed or weren't sure what to do next.

Whose approval a website is actually being designed for

A genuinely common pattern: a website gets designed to impress internal stakeholders, the business owner, the marketing team, in a presentation meeting, rather than to convert an actual visitor who arrives with a specific problem and limited patience. These are frequently different audiences with different needs, and a design optimised for the former doesn't automatically serve the latter well.

What actually builds trust instead of just visual polish

Clarity about what a business does and for whom, genuine evidence of results, straightforward pricing or process information, and an easy, obvious path to the next step build trust in a way that visual sophistication alone doesn't reliably achieve. A plainer site that's instantly clear and genuinely easy to act on frequently outperforms a more visually striking one that leaves a visitor genuinely unsure what to do next, or why they should even bother sticking around.

Where genuinely good design and conversion do align

This isn't an argument for a plain or unpolished site, good design absolutely still matters, for credibility, for brand perception, and for a genuinely pleasant overall experience. The distinction that matters most here is between design that genuinely serves clarity and conversion, and design that exists primarily to impress in a way disconnected from what an actual visitor genuinely needs to accomplish, two things frequently confused as being the same objective when they very much aren't.

A genuinely useful question to ask throughout any design process

Does this specific element help a visitor understand what we do and take the next step, or does it exist mainly because it looks sophisticated. Asking this consistently throughout a design process, element by element rather than only at the final review, tends to produce a site that converts noticeably better than one optimised primarily for how it performs in an internal presentation, and it's exactly the filter applied throughout every web design project rather than saved for a final critique after most decisions are already locked in.

A simple gut check worth trying

Show the homepage to someone unfamiliar with the business and ask what they think it's for. If the honest answer takes longer than a few seconds, or focuses on how it looks rather than what it does, that's a clearer signal than any internal design review that the balance has tipped too far toward impressiveness and away from clarity.
