Sometimes deleting
a page
is the SEO fix.

Content pruning sounds counterintuitive until the logic behind it becomes clear. Here's when it genuinely helps and when it doesn't.

By Santiago Payá de la Cruz• July 28, 2026• 7 min read

The instinct to never delete a page, since it might still bring in a stray visitor someday, is understandable but often wrong. A large volume of thin, outdated or duplicate pages doesn't just sit there neutrally, it can drag down how a search engine assesses the overall quality of a site, since average content quality across all indexed pages is genuinely part of what gets evaluated. Ten strong pages consistently outperform fifty mediocre ones competing for the same visibility. Deleting the right pages, done carefully, is sometimes the fastest available SEO fix.

The candidates worth pruning

Pages with essentially zero organic traffic over a meaningful period, content that's been superseded by a newer, more thorough piece on the same topic, and pages built purely to target a keyword with no real depth or reason for a reader to stay are the clearest candidates. A page that once ranked and has since been overtaken by competitors, and would need a substantial rewrite to be competitive again, is also worth weighing against simply starting fresh.

What genuinely shouldn't be pruned

A page with low traffic but genuine strategic value, supporting a specific product line or answering a niche but real question a subset of customers ask, shouldn't be deleted just because the traffic number looks unimpressive on a spreadsheet. Historical or evergreen reference content that occasionally earns backlinks, even without much direct traffic, often contributes more to overall site authority than its own traffic numbers suggest, which is exactly the kind of value a pure traffic-based review would miss entirely.

Delete, redirect or improve, the actual decision tree

If a page covers genuinely unique ground with real potential, improving it is almost always the better move than deleting it. If a very similar, stronger page already exists on the site, redirecting the weaker one preserves whatever backlinks or authority it accumulated rather than losing that value outright. Outright deletion, with a proper 410 or 404 response, is reserved for content that's genuinely redundant, outdated beyond repair, or was never valuable to begin with.

Doing this without accidentally damaging the site

Pruning without checking backlinks first risks losing real, hard-earned authority pointed at a page that gets deleted outright rather than redirected. Checking Search Console and a backlink tool before removing anything, and choosing a redirect over outright deletion whenever a page has earned meaningful external links, avoids turning a genuine improvement into a self-inflicted setback.

A realistic cadence for this kind of review

An annual content audit, reviewing traffic, backlinks and relevance across the full site, is a reasonable baseline for most businesses. Larger sites with hundreds or thousands of pages often benefit from a more frequent, rolling review focused on the lowest-performing segment rather than trying to review everything at once. This kind of audit is exactly the work that sits alongside a broader SEO audit, since pruning decisions depend on the same underlying data a technical review would already be pulling together. Treating pruning as routine maintenance rather than a rare, dramatic cleanup keeps it manageable and prevents the backlog that makes a first attempt feel overwhelming, the same way regular small tidying beats one dreaded annual deep clean.

The confidence that comes from doing this once, properly

The first pruning pass on a site that's never had one is always the hardest, since it means deciding on years of accumulated content at once. Every pass after that is smaller and faster, because the backlog never gets a chance to rebuild past a manageable size, which is the strongest argument for starting the first review sooner rather than waiting for a more convenient moment that rarely actually arrives.
