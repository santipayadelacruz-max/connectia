Core Web Vitals
and rankings:
the honest relationship.

Site speed gets treated as either a magic ranking lever or a distraction from real SEO. The truth sits in between.

By Santiago Payá de la Cruz• September 22, 2026• 8 min read

Core Web Vitals get discussed in two extreme framings: as a magic lever that will transform rankings once fixed, or as a distraction from the content and authority work that actually matters. Neither framing is accurate. Google has confirmed page experience, including Core Web Vitals, is a ranking factor, but it's explicitly one of many, and it functions more as a tiebreaker among otherwise comparable pages than as a force capable of overriding weak content or thin authority.

Where speed genuinely moves rankings

Among pages with broadly similar content quality and topical relevance, and this comparison is the one worth running before assuming speed alone explains a ranking gap, Core Web Vitals can meaningfully influence which one ranks higher, since Google explicitly built this signal to differentiate between pages that are otherwise close competitors for the same query. A page with excellent content but genuinely poor Core Web Vitals scores is competing with one hand behind its back against an equally strong competitor with a faster, more stable page.

Where speed alone won't compensate for weaker fundamentals

A technically fast page with thin, generic content won't outrank a slower page with genuinely authoritative, well-researched content covering the same topic more thoroughly. Content quality and topical authority remain the dominant factors by a wide margin, and treating speed optimisation as a substitute for actually improving content is one of the more common, avoidable strategic mistakes in SEO planning.

The business case that exists independent of rankings

Even setting the ranking impact aside entirely, Core Web Vitals correlate strongly with conversion rate, bounce rate and overall user satisfaction, all of which affect revenue directly regardless of where a page sits in search results. This is arguably the stronger, more reliable argument for prioritising it: a slow, janky page loses real business even on the rare occasion it manages to rank well despite the poor experience.

A sensible way to prioritise it within a broader SEO plan

Fixing genuinely poor Core Web Vitals scores, the kind that sit clearly in the red zone rather than borderline, deserves real priority since the gap between poor and good is where the ranking and conversion impact concentrate most heavily. Chasing marginal improvements from an already-good score to a perfect one, by contrast, rarely justifies the engineering time relative to content or link-building work that would move the needle further for the same investment. This is exactly the judgement call built into every technical SEO audit we run: fixing what's genuinely broken first, not chasing diminishing returns on an already-solid score.

What this means in practice for most businesses

Get Core Web Vitals out of the red and into at least the borderline-good range, then shift focus to content depth, topical authority and backlinks, which typically carry far more weight for competitive rankings. Revisit speed periodically as part of routine maintenance rather than treating it as a one-time project, since regressions creep back in easily as new features, scripts and images get added to a site over time without anyone specifically checking the performance impact.

Applying this in practice

Run a Core Web Vitals report today, note which pages sit clearly in the red, and start there rather than with an already-borderline page. That single prioritisation decision, red first, then borderline, then already-good, tends to produce the best return on the time invested, regardless of how the broader content and authority work is progressing in parallel.
