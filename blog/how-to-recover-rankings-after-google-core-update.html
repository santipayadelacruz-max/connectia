Rankings dropped
after a core update.
Now what?

A calm, practical process for diagnosing what actually happened and recovering, based on Google's own published guidance.

By Santiago Payá de la Cruz• April 7, 2026• 8 min read

A ranking drop that coincides with a Google core update triggers a very understandable urge to change everything immediately. That instinct is usually the wrong one, even though it rarely feels that way in the moment. Google's own guidance on core updates is explicit that a drop doesn't mean a site did anything wrong, and that panic edits to already-decent content can do more harm than good.

Step one: confirm it was actually the update

Check the Search Status Dashboard for the exact start and end dates of the rollout, then wait at least a full week after it completes before drawing conclusions, since rankings genuinely fluctuate during an active rollout. Compare the post-rollout week against the week before the update started, not against an arbitrary earlier period, and check whether the drop is isolated to specific pages or spread across the whole site.

Step two: size the drop before reacting

A small movement, dropping from position two to four, generally doesn't warrant drastic changes, and Google specifically advises against touching content that's still performing reasonably well. A large movement, dropping from position four to twenty-nine, deserves a proper investigation: which specific pages lost visibility, what do the pages now outranking them have that yours doesn't, and does the affected content genuinely satisfy what someone searching that term is looking for.

Step three: separate genuine quality issues from update noise

Not every drop that coincides with a core update is actually caused by it. Technical issues, a broken redirect, an accidental noindex tag, a migration that happened around the same time, can produce an identical-looking graph. Ruling these out first avoids the common mistake of rewriting content that was never actually the problem.

Step four: improve, don't just delete

Where content genuinely is thin or outdated, the fix is usually strengthening it, adding real depth, original analysis, first-hand expertise, rather than deleting it outright. Deletion is a last resort reserved for pages that were built purely to target a keyword rather than to serve an actual reader. A rushed round of mass content pruning right after a core update, without that distinction, is one of the more common overcorrections.

Step five: accept the timeline

Recovery isn't instant even after genuine improvements are made. Google's own guidance notes that the clearest gains often show up around the next core update rather than immediately, though smaller unannounced updates can produce partial movement in between. Planning for a multi-month timeline, rather than expecting a visible bounce within days, avoids the frustration that leads to more panic edits later. If a site's own SEO fundamentals were solid going in, patience is usually the harder part of recovery, not the technical work.

The mindset shift that actually helps

Treating a core update as a verdict on months or years of work tends to produce reactive, scattershot changes that don't actually address anything specific. Treating it as one data point in an ongoing process, worth investigating calmly and acting on deliberately, produces better outcomes almost every time. The sites that recover fastest are usually the ones that resisted the urge to do something, anything, in the first panicked week.
